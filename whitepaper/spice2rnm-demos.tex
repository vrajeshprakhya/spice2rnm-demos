\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Real number models are how a mixed-signal block joins a digital regression
suite. Writing one by hand is slow, and keeping it honest as the circuit
changes is slower. \textbf{spice2rnm} takes a SPICE netlist and produces a
synthesisable SystemVerilog real-number model, a UVM-MS verification
environment, and evidence that the two agree with the circuit --- in one
command, with no hand-written model and no hand-written testbench.

This paper walks through three blocks of increasing difficulty: a filter
whose behaviour is a frequency response, a duty-cycle corrector whose
behaviour is emphatically not, and a phase interpolator that has almost no
amplitude behaviour at all. The third one breaks the usual approach
outright, which is why it is here.

Every number in this document was produced by running the tool. Where a
result is unflattering it is printed anyway, because a verification tool
that only reports its wins is not a verification tool.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem}

A behavioural model has to be two contradictory things: fast enough to sit
in a digital regression, and faithful enough that passing it means
something. Hand-written models drift from their circuits, and the drift is
silent --- the model still compiles, still simulates, still passes.

The usual defence is a hand-written testbench, which has the same problem
one level up. Somebody decides what to check and how close is close enough,
and those decisions are rarely written down next to the number they
produced.

\textbf{spice2rnm} attacks both. It characterises the circuit in ngspice,
fits a structure to what it measured, emits SystemVerilog, and then emits a
UVM-MS environment whose golden values are \emph{ngspice measurements} ---
never the fitted equations. Checking a model against the equations it
implements passes unconditionally and means nothing; that rule is the spine
of everything below.

%======================================================================
\section{The pipeline}

\begin{lstlisting}
python3 -m spice2rnm dcc.cir --input-node clk --output-node vout \
        --emit-uvm-ms --spec dcc_spec.md
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent Seven stages run without further input:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Parse and repair} the netlist; supply missing model cards.
  \item \textbf{Partition} into blocks from the device graph, when the
        circuit has recoverable structure.
  \item \textbf{Characterise} each block in context: operating point, DC
        sweep, AC sweep, and --- where the dynamics move with level --- a
        large-signal step schedule.
  \item \textbf{Fit} a rational $H(s)$ and a static transfer curve.
  \item \textbf{Choose a structure} from what the measurements say, not
        from a fixed template (Section~\ref{sec:dcc}).
  \item \textbf{Emit} SystemVerilog, plus a UVM-MS environment with
        ngspice-measured goldens.
  \item \textbf{Prove it} by driving circuit and model with the same
        stimulus and comparing.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent An optional natural-language functional spec supplies what a
netlist cannot: what the block is \emph{for}, the rate it must run at, and
how close is close enough (Section~\ref{sec:spec}).

\section{Demo 1 --- Two-pole filter: the baseline}

An RC low-pass with two poles. This is the case the classical approach
handles well, and it is here to establish what ``working'' looks like
before the harder blocks complicate it.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Fitted response} & $2$ poles, $3.82\unit{MHz}$ and $26.2\unit{MHz}$ \\
AC fit residual          & $1.4\times10^{-8}\unit{dB}$ \\
DC curve                 & linear, residual $2.2\times10^{-16}$ \\
\midrule
Chirp equivalence        & $0.0290$ against a $0.15$ threshold \quad \pass \\
PRBS (independent stimulus) & $0.0097$ normalised \quad \pass \\
\midrule
UVM-MS, DC section       & $51$ checks, $0$ failed \\
UVM-MS, AC section       & $19$ checks, $0$ failed \\
\quad worst magnitude    & $0.0390\unit{dB}$ of $0.25$ at $50.1\unit{MHz}$ \\
\quad worst phase        & $2.70^{\circ}$ of $8.00^{\circ}$ at $708\unit{kHz}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Two details are worth more than the pass.

\textbf{The PRBS run is not the fit's own stimulus.} The model was fitted
against a chirp and is then checked against a pseudo-random bit sequence it
never saw. A model that had memorised its training signal would show it
here.

\textbf{One AC point was once wrong, and the fix was not to widen the
tolerance.} A $89\unit{MHz}$ point read $-38.63\unit{dB}$ where the model
computes $-38.30$. The model was right; the \emph{testbench} was driving at
$64$ samples per cycle while the model stepped at $303.663\unit{ps}$ ---
$36.9$ samples per cycle --- so it was missing every other update. Raising
the testbench's sampling rate fixed it. Widening the tolerance would also
have made the red go away, and would have hidden a real measurement bug.

\section{Demo 2 --- Duty-cycle corrector: when the usual approach fails}
\label{sec:dcc}

A comparator whose dominant pole moves $613{,}666\times$ across its input
range. Every fixed-pole structure fails on it, and each failure was
informative.

\subsection{Four structures}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Structure} & \textbf{Why it fails} & \textbf{Error} \\
\midrule
Additive, $\text{static} + (H - H(0))\!\cdot\! u$
  & correction term is $\pm24\unit{V}$ on a $1.8\unit{V}$ supply & $5.64$ \\
Wiener, $H_{\text{norm}}(s)[\,g(u)\,]$
  & bounded, but carries one bias point's poles & $0.337$ \\
Scheduled lag, rate from the AC sweep
  & measures an \emph{internal} node the output does not obey & $0.249$ \\
\textbf{Scheduled lag, rate from stepping}
  & \textbf{---} & $\mathbf{0.0769}$ \quad \pass \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent \textbf{The AC fit never moved more than $0.03\unit{dB}$ across
all four.} The frequency-domain residual could not distinguish a model that
leaves the supply rails from one that does not. That is the argument for
transient verification, stated as a measurement rather than as a principle.

The winning structure schedules its rate against a \emph{large-signal step}
measurement: step the input, time the output's own recovery. That spans
$2.1$ to $8.1\unit{GHz}$ of real rate, where the AC sweep reported six
orders of magnitude of the wrong node.

\subsection{The section that is deliberately absent}

The generated environment for this block has \emph{no AC section}. That is
not an omission, it is a measurement. Forced on:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
\toprule
All $10$ AC magnitude points & \fail, by $55.6$ to $62.1\unit{dB}$ \\
Model reads, flat across $3.16$--$112\unit{MHz}$ & $44.08\unit{dB}$ \\
ngspice reads over the same band & $-11.5$ to $-18.0\unit{dB}$ \\
\midrule
Same model, transient check & $0.0769$ \quad \pass \\
Same model, DC operating points & $51$ of $51$ \quad \pass \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Both are measurements of this circuit at this bias, and they
disagree by ${\sim}56\unit{dB}$, because they measure different things: the
model's small-signal gain at the bias is the static curve's \emph{local
slope}, not $H(0)$. Emitting that section would produce a testbench that
cannot pass --- and it costs more than the whole demo to say so, running
past $1200\unit{s}$ without reaching a verdict where the rest of the run
takes $72\unit{s}$.

\subsection{What replaces it}

A duty section: drive the circuit with clocks, record the output duty from
ngspice, drive the model with the same clocks. For a clock-path block that
is the function.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
\toprule
DC section    & $51$ checks, $0$ failed \\
Duty section  & $15$ checks, $0$ failed, worst $0.314$ pp of $0.500$ \\
Golden table spans & $20.1$ pp of output duty \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The span matters: a model that ignored its input entirely would
reproduce a flat table. This one cannot.

\subsection{A defect the new section found}

The model returned \emph{exactly} $50.000\%$ duty for a $50\%$ input,
whatever the circuit did. Structural, not a fitting error --- the rate was
indexed by input level alone, so the same level gave the same rate whichever
way the output travelled, and a symmetric curve driving a symmetric lag has
no asymmetry to give. The circuit has one: it falls in $239\unit{ps}$ and
rises in $334\unit{ps}$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
At $50\%$ input duty & \textbf{before} & \textbf{after} \\
\midrule
model                & $50.000\%$ & $49.855\%$ \\
ngspice              & $49.855\%$ & $49.855\%$ \\
error                & $0.148$ pp & $\mathbf{0.000}$ \textbf{pp} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Two things about this are worth stating plainly.

\textbf{The obvious fix did not work.} A rise/fall \emph{rate} asymmetry is
real and free to measure ($0.8005$, from the schedule's own endpoints) and
moved the worst error from $0.257$ to $0.256$ pp. Near the threshold the
scheduled rate is a $20\unit{ps}$ time constant, and $12\%$ of $20\unit{ps}$
is $2\unit{ps}$ against the $95\unit{ps}$ wanted. The edge position is set
by the static curve, not by the lag. The skew had to be emitted as a
\emph{delay}.

\textbf{The headline number got worse while the model got better.} Worst
duty error moved $0.258 \rightarrow 0.314$ pp, because a second defect --- a
${\sim}0.2$ pp edge-shape error, identical at both clock rates and so not a
delay --- had been partially masking the skew at $40/60\%$ duty. Removing
the skew stopped the masking. Both were always there; now one is fixed and
the other is measured and named.

\section{Demo 3 --- Phase interpolator: no frequency response at all}

A thermometer-coded phase interpolator has essentially no amplitude
transfer: measured gain $-0.03$ into a $39\unit{mV}$ excursion. There is no
$H(s)$ worth fitting, and an rms voltage comparison scores noise. The
classical pipeline stops at characterisation.

The block's specification is phase per code, DNL and INL --- so the tool
measures phase per code and emits a delay-line model,
$\text{out}(t) = \text{in}(t - \tau[\text{code}])$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
\toprule
Settings measured        & $9$ of $9$ \\
Total traversal          & $-500.1\unit{ps}$, monotonic \\
Mean step (1 LSB)        & $-62.51\unit{ps}$ \\
Worst DNL                & $-100.5\unit{ps}$ \quad ($-1.61$ LSB) \\
\midrule
UVM-MS phase section     & $9$ checks, $0$ failed \\
\quad worst              & $3.2627\unit{ps}$ of a $6.2513\unit{ps}$ tolerance \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The tolerance is a tenth of an LSB --- the delay line's own
quantisation floor, ten steps per LSB, so the check fails on a regression
and not on rounding. The environment is a different one from demos 1 and 2:
no DC section, no AC section, because neither an operating-point sweep nor
a small-signal linearisation describes edge placement.

The worst DNL of $-1.61$ LSB is a property of \emph{the circuit}, reported
faithfully. The model's job is to reproduce it, and it does.

\section{Why the green is worth believing}

A suite that passes everything is worth as much as one that fails
everything. Three mechanisms keep this one honest.

\textbf{Goldens are measurements, never the fit.} Every golden value in
every generated environment comes from ngspice. Checking a model against
its own equations passes unconditionally.

\textbf{Passing models are fault-injected, and the verdict must flip.}
Sensitivity is pinned from both sides --- a $1\%$ gain error must
\emph{not} be caught against a $15\%$ threshold, a $25\%$ one must be, and a
$2\times$ pole shift must be. For the duty section, measured on the emitted
model:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Injected fault} & \textbf{DC section} & \textbf{Duty section} \\
\midrule
comparator offset $+5\unit{mV}$   & caught & missed \\
comparator offset $+20\unit{mV}$  & caught & caught \\
pole schedule $\times0.5$         & \textcolor{fail}{blind} & missed \\
pole schedule $\times0.1$         & \textcolor{fail}{blind} & caught \\
pole schedule $\times0.02$        & \textcolor{fail}{blind} & caught \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Read honestly: the duty section is coarser than DC on offsets,
and a $2\times$ rate error still slips through. The row that justifies it is
the middle column --- DC samples settled values, so it is blind to
\emph{every} rate fault by construction, and this model has no valid AC
section. Without the duty half, nothing in that environment checks the
dynamics at all.

Two rows in this table moved when the rise/fall skew of
Section~\ref{sec:dcc} was fixed, and the direction is worth noting: the
$\times0.1$ rate fault was \emph{missed} by the pre-skew model's check,
because a systematic $0.25$ pp skew error was consuming the margin that now
exposes it. Fixing a model defect made the checker sharper --- the two are
not independent, and a fault table is only as current as the model it was
measured on. This one was re-measured on the shipped model.

\textbf{No circuit has to stay broken.} An earlier version of the suite
depended on one case continuing to fail, as a canary. That decayed twice in
one day as the tool improved. Fault injection replaced it.

\section{The functional spec, and what it is really for}
\label{sec:spec}

A netlist says what a circuit \emph{is}. It never says what it is
\emph{for}, what rate it must run at, or how close is close enough. Those
were defaults the tool invented for itself --- and two were poor: a clock
rate derived from the fitted poles, and a duty tolerance chosen by
measuring what a model could already do, which is close to circular.

\texttt{--spec} reads a plain-language specification and fills them in:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
 & \textbf{invented} & \textbf{from the spec} \\
\midrule
Clock rates      & $14.8$, $29.5\unit{MHz}$ & $50$, $119.5\unit{MHz}$ \\
Input duties     & $40/50/60\%$ & $35/50/65\%$ \\
Duty tolerance   & $0.500$ pp & $1.000$ pp \\
Golden span      & $20.1$ pp & $30.7$ pp \\
\midrule
Verdict          & \multicolumn{2}{l}{$15$ checks, $0$ failed, worst $0.428$ pp \quad \pass} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent \textbf{But the most valuable output is disagreement:}

\begin{lstlisting}
SPEC CONFLICT: the spec says this block operates up to 4e+08 Hz, but
the fitted model is only valid below 1.195e+08 Hz -- 3.3x lower.
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent Either the characterisation must be run wider or the spec's range
is out of scope. That is a question for an engineer, not something a tool
should absorb --- so the measurement wins, the check is held at the band,
and the gap is reported.

\textbf{What a spec will not do} is improve a number the netlist can be
measured for. The rise/fall skew of Section~\ref{sec:dcc} was found by
stepping the circuit and timing the output. A sentence claiming ``typical
propagation delay $300\unit{ps}$'' would have been worse than that
measurement --- and indeed the real spec states the asymmetry exists
without giving a number, which is how specifications are actually written.

\section{Where the model in the loop sits}

Language models are used at three points, and every one of them follows the
same rule: \textbf{intent from the model, physics from the measurement.} A
proposal that does not survive validation against the measured circuit is
discarded, and the run continues on the default it would have used anyway.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=0.3em]
  \item \textbf{Partitioning} --- proposing block boundaries in the device
        graph.
  \item \textbf{Block function} --- what the block is for, which selects
        the stimulus, the scoring metric, the verification environment, and
        the model structure itself.
  \item \textbf{Specification reading} --- extracting the handful of facts
        from prose that change how a model is built.
\end{itemize}

\noindent On the duty-cycle corrector the advisor classified the block
correctly from measured evidence, independently identified a degenerate
artefact in the fitted response, and exposed a wrong gate in the tool's own
validator. It also once chose a verification environment that requires a
discrete control the block does not have --- which the validator refused,
with the reason printed. Both outcomes are the system working.

\section{Limits}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=0.3em]
  \item \textbf{Composition costs accuracy.} On a two-block feedback
        amplifier both blocks pass individually, at $0.0748$ and $0.1366$,
        while the composed system misses at $0.1995$. The cascade of the
        two in-context fits matches the real circuit to $0.023\unit{dB}$
        with no systematic bias, so this is error propagating through gain,
        not double-counted loading --- checked, not assumed.
  \item \textbf{The duty check is coarse on offsets}, as the fault table
        above shows.
  \item \textbf{An edge-shape error of ${\sim}0.2$ pp} remains in the
        duty-cycle corrector's model, independent of clock rate and so not
        a delay. It is measured, named, and unfixed.
  \item \textbf{Loops the device graph cannot represent} --- and loops that
        are never in the netlist at all --- are outside what structural
        partitioning can recover.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reproducing this}

\begin{lstlisting}
git clone <spice2rnm>  &&  git clone <spice2rnm-demos>
cd demos && ./run_all.sh
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent Requires ngspice and an Icarus-compatible simulator with UVM
support. \texttt{--spec} and the advisors additionally need an Anthropic API
key; without one, every advisor declines cleanly and the pipeline runs on
its measured defaults.
